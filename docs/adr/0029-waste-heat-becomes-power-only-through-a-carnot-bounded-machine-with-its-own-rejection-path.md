# Waste heat becomes power only through a Carnot-bounded machine with its own rejection path

Status: accepted (#724 G6, #803–#808)

Until the Heat engine existed, a thermal reservoir had exactly two fates: it rejected to space through a radiator, or it did not and its heat accumulated. Nothing in the model could take a temperature difference and hand electricity back to the bus, which is the audit finding G6 in #724 — a station that could not do the one thing that makes waste heat a resource rather than a liability. The gap was easy to fill badly, because the tempting implementations are all conservation-clean on paper and physically false. Six decisions separate what was built from what the shortcut would have produced.

**Heat-to-power is a component with a body, not a discount on the rejection number.** The cheapest way to "recover" waste heat is to author the radiator smaller and credit the difference as recovered electricity: the ledger closes, the budget balances, and nothing in the model contradicts it. That is exactly the fiction this ADR exists to forbid. Recovery in this model is a `HeatEngineSettings` entry with a hot reservoir, a rejection reservoir, a rated thermal intake, a Carnot fraction, an auxiliary draw and two temperature gates, and it is the machine — not an author's arithmetic — that decides how much of a gradient becomes watts. A recovery credit with no machine between the hot node and the bus is a leak with good bookkeeping, and the component's existence is what makes that leak impossible to author.

**Efficiency is a declared fraction of the Carnot ceiling at the interface temperatures, enforced at authoring time.** The usable efficiency each tick is `carnotFraction × (1 − T_c/T_k)`, evaluated at the rejection reservoir's and hot reservoir's absolute temperatures for that tick, and `carnotFraction` is validated into `(0, 1]`. A fraction above one is refused at load rather than clamped at runtime, so a station can never be authored more efficient than the second law allows and then have the simulator quietly discover the limit on its behalf. The consequence is that efficiency is not a constant: the same machine recovers more on a cold morning than a warm one, and the demo's throttle curve is that fact rather than a modelling artefact. Because the ceiling is evaluated at the *interface* temperatures, the author is honest about where the machine's own irreversibility sits — inside the machine, as a fraction, rather than smuggled into the reservoir temperatures.

**A heat engine needs two reservoirs and two radiators, and the rejection reservoir's class is set by where the rejection arrives, not by where the heat came from.** This is the decision the recovery story most reliably loses. Recovering power from a hot node does not make the heat go away; it makes *less* of it, and what remains arrives at the rejection reservoir at a temperature the author must state. In the reference bottoming cycle the generator's sink is hot and the engine's rejection lands at 5–45 °C, which is the `moderate` service class, not the `low` class the chilled loop occupies and not the hot class of the source it was fed from. Getting that wrong is not cosmetic: thermal-class compatibility is what stops rejection heat from being booked into a loop that cannot carry it, and the class is a statement about the destination. The reference station's `engineReject` reservoir is the first `moderate`-class member it has, and its pairing with the engine is validated against the engine's `thermalClass` at load.

**The engine converts only heat whose power actually landed.** Net output leaves through the engine's generation connection, and the power the connection actually delivers caps the whole conversion: a refusal at the cable shrinks the intake back down so the unconverted heat stays in the hot reservoir rather than being booked as electricity that went nowhere. The same discipline governs the bus side through the acceptance-coupled throttle — the engine asks for its net output and the station admits only the demand presented to the pool plus the charge headroom the batteries can still take, less generation already ahead of it. What is not admitted is not converted. An engine that extracted heat to produce power the bus refused would be a heat pump running for no reason, and the limiter names `DemandAcceptance` or `GenerationConnection` so the shortfall is legible instead of mysterious.

**Where the machine cannot work, it idles; where its rejection path fails, it trips.** Five distinct refusals are named rather than collapsed into one: `NoGradient` at `T_h ≤ T_c`, where Carnot efficiency is zero or negative and the alternative would be negative power or pumping heat cold-to-hot; `TooCold` below the authored minimum hot temperature, which is the source not being worth the machine's while rather than anything wrong with the machine; `AuxiliaryPower` when gross output cannot cover the machine's own parasitic draw, so it does not become a load dressed as a generator; `DemandAcceptance` and `GenerationConnection` above; and `SinkOver`, where a rejection reservoir at or above `maxSinkTemperatureC` refuses the rejection heat and the engine trips whole rather than forcing heat where it cannot go. The trip is re-evaluated every tick and clears itself when the sink falls back below the gate. Every one of these is a case where a plausible implementation would have produced a number; the machine produces a status instead, and books nothing.

**The hot-side supply cap stops the engine from creating the ordering violation it needs to keep running.** Over a tick the engine may draw at most `C_hot × (T_h − T_c)` — the heat its hot reservoir actually holds above the rejection reservoir's tick-start temperature. Without that cap a rated intake could drive the hot node below its own sink mid-step, and the engine would then be running uphill on a gradient it manufactured itself. The cap bounds the engines' *aggregate* contribution; other processes may still move the hot node, and the engines simply find less available next tick. Engines sharing a source share one budget per integration interval (#826): its floor is the warmest tick-start sink among requesting (`commandedInputKW > 0`), in-service, temperature-qualified engines (positive gradient, hot gate met, sink gate clear). This deliberately conservative reservation remains even if a candidate later cannot cover auxiliaries or deliver power. Declaration order retains the existing allocation priority, but cannot change that common budget, for equal or unequal sink temperatures. Each engine receives only the budget remaining after actual reservoir-side withdrawals by earlier engines, including intake transport losses; throttled or refused conversion spends no unwithdrawn heat. Incoming heat and engine rejection do not replenish the tick-start budget within the interval. Zero-command, disabled, out-of-service and temperature-refused engines do not reserve a floor.

The reference station carries the whole story as its demo: `orcBottoming` takes the generator's waste-heat sink as its hot side, rejects into a dedicated `engineReject` buffer with its own panel, and recovers a real share of the generator's 27.86 kW full-load waste heat rather than a token trickle. Its rejection buffer is deliberately a *saturating* one — 400 kJ/K behind a 1 m² panel, well short of what would carry 31 kW in steady state — because a properly sized rejection radiator would never trip, and the trip is half the point: it is what shows the rejection path as a real capacity that can run out. The demo therefore declares its hot state at the start rather than spending ten simulated hours earning it, since a 20,000 kJ/K generator sink warms at about 1.4 mK/s under generator waste heat and no choreography is legible at that rate.

Reversing any of these reintroduces the gap under a different name. Crediting recovery without a machine restores the leak. Clamping `carnotFraction` at runtime instead of refusing it lets a station be authored second-law-breaking and corrected silently. Choosing the rejection reservoir's class from the source rather than the destination breaks class-compatibility guarantees that other subsystems rely on. Skipping the delivered-power cap books electricity that never arrived. Collapsing the five refusals into one status hides the difference between a cold source, a saturated sink, a closed bus and a machine that costs more than it makes — four different operator actions wearing one badge. And sizing the reference rejection radiator for steady state would close the demo by hiding the only failure mode worth showing.
